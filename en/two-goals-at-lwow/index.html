Two goals at Lwów.

In 1942, a man played football in a forced labor camp. He scored twice.

Times of Israel · July 4, 2026

David Bolchover spent years reconstructing the careers of eleven Jewish professional footballers — among the finest players in Europe before the war — whose lives and records were destroyed with them. József Braun, described by Bolchover as the greatest Jewish footballer who ever lived, made his debut for the Hungarian national team at seventeen. His name is unknown outside the research. Józef Klotz scored Poland's first-ever goal in international competition — a penalty against Sweden in Stockholm, in May 1922, in only the third international match his country had ever played. A photograph of his daughter has been recovered. Her name is not known. Her fate is not known. Zygmunt Steuermann, who scored the first hat-trick ever recorded by a Polish international, was deported and put to forced labor at the Janowska camp in Lwów. According to a postwar account cited by Bolchover, he played a match inside the camp and scored twice. It was his last recorded game.

Bolchover's book is called Digging Deep. He began the research believing, as he puts it, that Jews were not great footballers — until he read something about Béla Guttmann, and found he had been wrong.

The two goals at Lwów are not a symbol of resistance. That framing would require the camp to be the primary fact, and the football to be the response to it. But Steuermann did not score against the camp. He scored against a goalkeeper. The match had rules. Someone kept the score. There were two sides. Whatever the ground looked like, whatever surrounded it, inside the ninety minutes — or however long it lasted — there was a game being played, and he was playing it, and he scored twice.

The camp was the context. It was not the content.

This is a harder distinction than it sounds. We are trained, looking back at those years, to let the context swallow everything inside it. Every act that took place there becomes an act about being there. The football becomes defiance, or testimony, or a symptom of something. It cannot simply be football — a man doing what he was good at, in the only time and place available to him, completely.

But that is what it was.

There is a way of understanding action in which the conditions surrounding an act are finally irrelevant to the nature of the act itself. Not irrelevant to the person performing it — Steuermann knew where he was; he was not deceived about his situation. Irrelevant to what the act is. A goal scored in Lwów in 1942 is a goal. Its coordinates do not alter its category. The thing accomplished is the thing accomplished, entire, independent of what frames it. The frame can be documented. It can be mourned. It does not reach inside.

The tradition has a word for this kind of completeness — the quality of an act that is fully itself regardless of what surrounds it. It is not heroism. Heroism is a relationship with adversity. This is something prior to that relationship: the simple fact that certain things, when they occur, occur completely. They do not wait for better conditions. They do not require a different context in order to be what they are. They happen, and in happening they are finished, and nothing that follows can revise them.

Steuermann's two goals cannot be taken back. They are recorded — thinly, barely, by Bolchover's research — but their existence does not depend on the record. They happened in Lwów, in 1942, inside a camp, in a match with rules and a keeper and a score. They are as complete as any goal scored in a stadium with a crowd.

The book recovers something more stubborn than achievement, more stubborn than correction. It recovers the fact that these men played, and scored, and won, and lost, in the full sense of those words — and that the full sense of those words is not conditional on what came after.

Klotz scored Poland's first international goal in 1922. That is a fact of the same order as any other fact of that kind. The decades of silence around it do not reach back and unmake the moment. The goal stood then. It stands now. It will stand when this article is forgotten.

The context was the camp. The game was the game.
