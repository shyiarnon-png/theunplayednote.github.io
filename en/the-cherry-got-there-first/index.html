The cherry got there first.

On a hillside above the Lebanese border, a farmer is picking fruit under rocket fire. His stress has nothing to do with the war.

Ynet / Maariv · May 2026

When the war broke out in October 2023, Yoni Yakobi didn't imagine he would be opening cherry season eighteen months later under fire. His farm sits at Margaliot — a moshav pressed against the Lebanese border, close enough that its houses are visible from the other side. Seven dunams of cherry trees. Three tons of fruit in a normal year.

This is not a normal year.

In January, when the trees needed to be sprayed and coaxed into their annual awakening, the army told him he couldn't enter his orchards. For a month, the trees waited without him. They woke late, incompletely, and the yield dropped. Then the foreign workers he depends on for the harvest didn't come: the government closed the skies, and the Thai labourers who pick cherries every spring across the Galilee couldn't get in.

He picks anyway. With whoever he can find. Under whatever conditions exist that morning.

"I work with so much stress," he said, "that I don't even have the possibility of thinking about the situation."

The cherries are ripening. The rockets are falling. And somewhere on a hillside above the Lebanese border, a man is reaching into a tree.

Read that sentence again. Slowly.

He is not saying: despite the war, I keep working. That would be a story about courage — a man pushing through difficulty, holding on against the odds. We know that story. It is a good story. It is not this one.

What Yoni Yakobi is saying is something structurally different. He is saying: the cherry is so present, so immediate, so real in its demands — the yield, the workers, the timing, the weight of each piece of fruit — that the war has not found a place to land. Not because the war isn't there. It is there. The rockets are audible from his orchard. But the cherry got there first.

This is the thing the headline cannot say about itself: the stress is not the war. The stress is the harvest. The war is the background. The cherry is the foreground. And in that inversion — quiet, unremarkable, reported almost as an aside — lies something that takes a lifetime to understand and almost no one manages to live.

The opposite of fear is not courage. Courage still knows the threat is primary. The opposite of fear — the real opposite, the structural opposite — is presence. The state in which something living is so fully occupying your attention that the threat, real as it is, cannot find the centre of the room. It finds the edges. It makes noise at the edges. But the centre belongs to the cherry.

The Chassidic tradition calls this tov — the good, the living, the generative — as the most fundamental layer of reality. Not more powerful than danger. Not safer. More real. The cherry tree does not know there is a war. It knows it is May. It knows the light is long and the ground is warm and the fruit is ready. And the man who tends it, in the act of tending it, enters for a moment into that knowledge.

He is not escaping the war. He is remembering, through his hands, what precedes it.

The rockets will fall or they won't. The yield will be low. The workers didn't come. The trees woke late. Everything that could go wrong has gone somewhat wrong — and the cherries are ripening anyway, on a hillside above Lebanon, in the hands of a man too busy with life to be consumed by its interruption.

That is not a detail. That is the whole story.
